Title: How to Build a Business That Is Yours (Co-Creator Identity Algorithm)

Meta: Pick a business you will keep working on with no budget. The methodology's Co-Creator Identity Algorithm, with GaryVee's jigsaw-puzzle example as the worked case.

## The problem this solves

You want to start something. You have a job, no money to hire anyone, and an idea you keep putting off because you think you need more resources first. When you do sit down to choose what to build, you reach for whatever category people say the money is in, and a month later you are tired of it and have not started.

The methodology's Co-Creator Identity Algorithm is for exactly this. Use when: identifying purpose, work, or contribution that is structurally yours rather than culturally assigned. Read through the methodology, GaryVee's advice in "How I'd Grow a Business With $0 in 2026" is this algorithm run on one person's business: start from an interest you already have, find where it meets something other people want, and let that meeting point decide what you make.

## What the methodology says

Layer IV, the Co-Creator Identity Algorithm. Use when: identifying purpose, work, or contribution that is structurally yours rather than culturally assigned.

1. Identify your most selfish creative desire. The one with no ceiling. What would you build if no constraint existed? Must be generative, not consumptive.
2. Identify your most selfless creative desire. The one that does not exist yet but should. What would you make possible for others? Must be creative, not merely sacrificial.
3. Find their convergence. Per Polarity: if both are genuine extremes, they must converge.
4. Test the convergence point. Does the most selfish form require service to others? Does the most selfless form produce personal fulfillment? Both must be true.
5. The convergence point is your structural purpose. Not aspirational. Derived.
6. Apply the filter. Every decision now passes through: does this compound toward the convergence point, or away from it?
7. Per the Five Modes of Propagation: choose how the work outlasts you. Direct teaching, demonstration, documentation, system-building, cultural embedding. Most lasting work uses all five.

## How to run it in your business

Applied, not canon: the steps above are the methodology's. What follows is one way to run them when you are alone, after work, with no budget. Each step is read as DETECT (notice the signal), PROCESS (do the work), RESPOND (act on the result).

### Step 1 - Identify your most selfish creative desire

Correspondence (this article's reading): Gary's "what do I voluntarily spend time doing, what subject do I keep talking about without needing a reward" is the raw material for this step. The step then narrows it: the methodology asks what you would build, not only what you enjoy, and says it must be generative, not consumptive (making something, not only taking something in).

What you do: write down the activities and subjects you return to with nobody pushing you. Then, beside each, write what you would make around it if nothing stopped you. Gary's puzzle example does this in one move: enjoying puzzles is consumptive, filming yourself doing them and sharing the clips is the generative version of the same interest. What it produces: a short list of interests, each with a thing you would make. Who does it: you, alone, before anyone else is involved.

DETECT: notice what you already spend evenings on with no reward attached. PROCESS: turn each interest into something you would build from it. RESPOND: keep the ones that have a build in them, and cross out the ones that are only consumption.

### Step 2 - Identify your most selfless creative desire

Correspondence (this article's reading): Gary addresses half of this step. "Build toward a business that fits you" and "create something other people want" is the service side; he does not frame it as the thing that does not exist yet but should. That part is the methodology's, and it is what makes the step more than market research.

What you do: for the interests left from step 1, write what you could make possible for other people who share them, and specifically what nobody is making for them yet. The methodology says creative, not merely sacrificial: not "give my time away" but "make the thing". What it produces: one or two sentences per interest naming a gap. Who does it: you, but this is the step where you can ask the people who share the interest what they wish existed.

DETECT: notice what people in that interest complain is missing. PROCESS: name the thing you could make that fills it. RESPOND: write it down beside the selfish desire from step 1.

### Step 3 - Find their convergence

Correspondence (this article's reading): Gary's puzzle-and-microbrews example is the closest thing in the video to this step, with one difference worth saying. His convergence is of two interests of yours ("the combination might become the thing people remember"); the methodology's is of what you want to make and what others need made. His point still holds for the step: the overlap is more distinctive than either side alone.

What you do: lay the step 1 list beside the step 2 list and look for the pair that is the same thing described twice. The methodology says, per Polarity (the principle that genuine opposites are two ends of one thing), that if both desires are genuine extremes they must converge. If nothing converges, one of the two lists is not honest yet; go back a step. What it produces: one sentence describing a thing to make that you want to make and that others need. Who does it: you.

DETECT: notice where the two lists describe the same thing in different words. PROCESS: write that thing in one sentence. RESPOND: if there is no such sentence, return to step 1 or 2 rather than forcing one.

### Step 4 - Test the convergence point

Correspondence (this article's reading): Gary's "Do I want to do the work, or do I only want the outcome I imagine comes with it?" is the fulfillment test. His "posting isn't the same as people caring, and people caring isn't automatically the same as people buying" is the service test. Both halves are in the video.

What you do: ask the two questions the methodology asks, and require both to be yes. Does the selfish form require serving others? If you could make this and nobody ever saw it, would it still be the thing? Then it fails the first test. Does the selfless form produce your own fulfillment? If you would make it only because it is needed, Gary's evening problem applies: there is no energy to come back to it. What it produces: a yes or a no. Who does it: you, honestly.

DETECT: notice which of the two questions you want to skip. PROCESS: answer that one first. RESPOND: a double yes moves to step 5; anything else is a signal to revise, not to push through.

### Step 5 - The convergence point is your structural purpose

Correspondence (this article's reading): "Your niche is you. Other people can choose the same topic; they can't become you by selecting the same category." That is Gary's version of a purpose that is derived rather than chosen.

What you do: write the convergence sentence from step 3 at the top of the page where you plan the business, and treat it as the business's definition. The methodology says not aspirational, derived: it is not a mission statement you hope to live up to, it is the thing that was already true once you did steps 1 to 4 honestly. What it produces: the one line that every later decision is checked against. Who does it: you.

DETECT: notice the pull to make the sentence sound more impressive. PROCESS: leave it in plain words. RESPOND: pin it where you will see it before every working session.

### Step 6 - Apply the filter

Correspondence (this article's reading): Gary's "people chase crypto, then real estate, then cannabis, now AI" and "be honest about that before you build your life around it" is this step applied to the category decision. His warning not to add beer to puzzle content "because you think I've handed you a formula" is the same filter applied to content decisions.

What you do: before each decision about the business, ask the methodology's one question: does this compound toward the convergence point, or away from it? Which platform to post on, what to make next, whether to take a sponsorship, whether to add a second interest to the content. What it produces: faster decisions, and a written list of the things you said no to and why. Who does it: you, every time, for as long as the business exists.

DETECT: notice an opportunity that is attractive mainly because someone else is excited about it. PROCESS: run it through the one question. RESPOND: take what compounds toward the point, decline what moves away from it, and write down the decline.

### Step 7 - Choose how the work outlasts you

Correspondence (this article's reading): Gary covers two of the five modes directly and gestures at a third. "Make the activity visible" by filming the puzzle is demonstration; the clips themselves are documentation; the "Jigsaw Jane" community that could later support merchandise, brand deals and an appearance is cultural embedding. He does not address direct teaching or system-building; the methodology says most lasting work uses all five.

What you do: name which of the five modes your business already uses and which it does not. Direct teaching (teaching one person), demonstration (showing the work), documentation (encoding it in text, video or structured media), system-building (making it run without you), cultural embedding (making it an assumption in a community). What it produces: a five-row list with a yes or no beside each. Who does it: you; the two modes Gary does not cover are the ones most people skip.

DETECT: notice which modes your current plan leans on entirely. PROCESS: pick one missing mode to add. RESPOND: add it to the plan, dated.

## Three places to use it this week

Applied, not canon: the video is about one person's content business. The algorithm is not, so two of these three run it where Gary did not.

### In operations

Not covered in this video. Gary's food-maker anecdote mentions shipping nationally and nothing about how. To run the algorithm on operations you would apply step 6 to every process decision: does hiring this person, buying this tool, or adding this step compound toward the convergence point? The one decision you would have to make first is what "the point" is for your operation, which is steps 1 to 5 run on the business rather than on you. Your first artifact is that one sentence for the business.

### In marketing

You enjoy puzzles and know microbrews, and your clips currently show only the puzzles. DETECT: notice that the beer knowledge is a real interest you have been hiding, which is Gary's "sand off every interesting edge" warning. PROCESS: run step 3 on the pair; if the combination is one thing described twice, make the next clip with both in it. RESPOND: watch which part people respond to, as Gary says, without deciding in advance which it will be. Your first artifact is a one-line content brief: the activity, the second interest, and the question you are testing.

### In growth and sales

You are publishing and want to know what the business could sell. DETECT: notice which of Gary's named paths fits the convergence sentence: your own product, merchandise, sponsorships, appearances. PROCESS: run step 6 on each; a path that moves away from the point is declined even if it pays. RESPOND: pick the one that compounds, and list what you would need to learn before pursuing it. Outreach, pricing and deal terms are Not covered in this video. Your first artifact is one chosen revenue path and its open questions.

## How you will know it is working, and how you will know it is not

Working:

- You keep making the thing after work without manufacturing the motivation, which Gary names as the practical reason to start from interest.
- Decisions get faster because step 6 answers them, and your list of declined opportunities grows.
- People respond to a specific detail of yours, not to the category, which is Gary's "your niche is you" showing up in the response.

Not working:

- The video does not name one. A reasonable one to set yourself: pick a review date now, and if by then you have stopped making the thing, or every decision has been made on what others were excited about rather than on the convergence sentence, the convergence point was not honest and steps 1 to 4 need running again.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a category because the money is said to be there. Gary names crypto, real estate, cannabis and AI as categories people chase without caring about the work.
- Manufacturing a combination. Gary: do not add beer to puzzle content because it sounds like a formula; the overlap only works if both interests are real.
- Quitting after three posts. Gary uses a two-year horizon to make the point that a handful of clips is not a verdict.
- Treating anything short of a full-time career as failure. Gary's point about an extra $5,000 a year is that it is money you did not have before.
- Sanding off your edges to look like everyone else in the category.

## Start here

Applied, not canon: tonight, do step 1 only. Write down the three things you spend evenings on without being asked, and beside each, the thing you would make from it if nothing stopped you. Stop there. Do not plan merchandise, a sponsor, or a name. Your starting artifact is three lines on a page.

## Key terms

- Co-Creator Identity: the methodology's name for work that is structurally yours, derived from where what you want to make meets what others need made.
- Generative, not consumptive: making something rather than only taking something in; the test step 1 applies to a desire.
- Convergence: the point where your selfish and selfless creative desires turn out to describe one thing.
- Polarity: the principle that genuine opposites are two ends of one thing, which is why two honest extremes converge.
- Five Modes of Propagation: the five ways a process outlasts its creator, from teaching one person to becoming a community's assumption.
- DETECT, PROCESS, RESPOND: the methodology's one shape for every process: notice the signal, do the work, act on the result.

## Questions this video answers

How can I start a business with no money to hire anyone? Gary's answer is to publish around something you already do, through social media, before any team exists. He names a food maker who began posting on Instagram and TikTok and, he says, was two years later taking home $750,000 a year shipping her product nationally; he presents it as one person's story, not a forecast.

What if my interest is too ordinary to build on? Gary uses jigsaw puzzles as the example on purpose. The interest does not have to be impressive; it has to be one you will keep returning to and can show.

How much could a hobby business make? Gary gives two illustrations and calls both hypothetical: $5,000 a year in puzzle-company brand deals as a modest outcome, and a "Jigsaw Jane" case with $180,000 in T-shirt sales, $50,000 in brand deals and a $10,000 convention appearance. He says merchandise sales are not take-home income.

How do I stand out when everyone is on social media? Gary says to get more specific, not to wait for an empty platform, and to let real secondary interests show: puzzles and microbrews rather than puzzles alone.

Do I need to be good at the thing first? Gary says liking something and being skilled at it are different, that he ranked 243 out of 251 in his high school class and builds businesses anyway, and that you can get better at what you enjoy.
